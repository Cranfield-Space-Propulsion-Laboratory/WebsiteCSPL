Cranfield Space Centre

Future propulsion systems for Earth orbit and beyond.

The Cranfield Space Propulsion Laboratory (CSPL) designs, models, and tests propulsion systems for the spacecrafts of the future, focusing on electric thrusters, alongside chemical rocket propulsion and critical support systems. CSPL academics are part of Cranfield University Space Centre and teach the modules on Space Propulsion and Space Electric Propulsion for the Astronautics and Space Engineering MSc.

What we do

The lab of space propulsion.

While electric propulsion is the lab's predominant focus, built around an expertise in electrospray thrusters, all forms of in-space propulsion are being pursued with forays in chemical propulsion projects such as detonation-based engines, liquid rocket engines, or storage of hydrogen for long-term missions.

01

Electric Propulsion

High-performance thrusters for spacecrafts, and the systems that keep them fed, from multiplexed electrospray arrays for Smallsats to active cryogenic intakes for Atmosphere-Breathing Electric Propulsion.

  • Cryogenic propellant regeneration for ABEP
  • Multiplexed electrospray thruster manufacturing
  • ML-driven molecular propellant characterisation
  • Metallic Plasma Thrusters
More on Electric Propulsion →
02

Chemical Propulsion

High-thrust systems for launch, landing and orbital manoeuvres, built and fired in Cranfield's own test warehouse.

  • Rotating detonation engines
  • Long-term hydrogen storage
  • Liquid & monopropellant rockets
  • Electro-fluidic injectors

Research highlights

Three current highlighted projects.

Electric Propulsion

Cryogenic Regeneration

A cryopump-based intake that captures and stores atmospheric propellant between orbits, decoupling collection from thrust for air-breathing electric propulsion — one of the pillars of CSPL's EP programme.

Electric Propulsion

Electrospray Manufacturing

Multiplexing hundreds of emission sites into compact arrays, and developing low-cost porous materials to replace costly sintered metals and glass substrates.

ML Propellant Characterisation
